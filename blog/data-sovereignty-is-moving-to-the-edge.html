Data sovereignty is moving to the edge

A ministry will not put its workloads in someone else's region. A router in the living room still phones home. Nations are pulling AI to the metal. You can too.

A router that already sits in millions of living rooms can now report motion history to the provider’s cloud. Opt-in language does not change the architecture. The record leaves the house. A ministry will not put its workloads in someone else’s region. That is the week.

In the first half of 2026, the CNAS Sovereign AI Index counted more new national projects than in all of 2024. The running total sits at 184 projects across 67 countries. Governments are no longer treating AI as something you rent from a foreign provider. They are treating the stack itself as infrastructure that must stay under local control.

It is not isolated. Google expanded on-prem and air-gapped options in Distributed Cloud for customers who need data residency. AI security cameras are shifting inference onto the device so footage does not have to leave the building — one analysis put the cost reduction near 67 percent. The same window produced enforcement reminders: a near-billion-euro GDPR fine against Uber in the Netherlands and a $400 million U.S. settlement with TikTok over children’s privacy. Jurisdiction is being enforced more visibly, not less.

The people with the most at stake are pulling compute and data closer to the metal. The personal question is whether you will do the same.

The asymmetry at home

Critical infrastructure is being designed so sensitive material stays local by default. Consumer systems still default to “phone home.” Nations fund their own models. Enterprises buy air-gapped capacity so regulated data never crosses a border they do not control. Even surveillance vendors now advertise edge processing, because sending every frame to the cloud is expensive and politically awkward.

The technology to reverse the consumer default already exists. Local models. Local graphs. Apps that run as demos in the browser and as the real product on your machine.

The personal version of the same move

If a ministry will not put its workloads in someone else’s region, there is little reason an individual should put a lifetime of photos, notes, conversations, and agents in someone else’s chat log by default. Keep the data. Run the model where you can. Decide what leaves.

That is the design of the Sci-Fi Labs stack. The Knowledge Graph is a private spatial canvas for memory — photos, conversations, time as depth — on hardware you control. Where is Paul? turns a personal travel record into a globe and timeline without parking the raw material on a third-party server. The published sites are demos. The product is the copy that runs on your machine.

None of this requires a national AI fund. It requires treating personal data with the same seriousness the CNAS index is already applying to national data.
