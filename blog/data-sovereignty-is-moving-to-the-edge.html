Data sovereignty is moving to the edge

Governments, enterprises, and even surveillance hardware are pulling AI closer to the metal. Cloud dependency is now treated as a strategic risk — and the personal version of that move is already possible.

In the first half of 2026, the CNAS Sovereign AI Index counted more new national projects than in all of 2024. The running total sits at 184 projects across 67 countries. Governments are no longer treating AI as something you rent from a foreign provider. They are treating the stack itself as infrastructure that must stay under local control.

That is the strongest signal of the week. It is not isolated. Google expanded on-prem and air-gapped options in Distributed Cloud specifically for customers who need data residency. AI security cameras are shifting inference onto the device so footage does not have to leave the building — one analysis put the cost reduction near 67 percent while improving sovereignty for critical sites. At the same time, a major ISP turned existing home gateways into motion sensors and stores up to seven days of activity in its own cloud, disclosable under legal process.

The people with the most at stake are pulling compute and data closer to the metal. The personal question is whether you will do the same.

Sovereignty stopped being a slogan

For years, “data sovereignty” lived mostly in policy papers and procurement checklists. The recent cluster of moves makes it concrete. Nations fund their own models and compute. Enterprises buy air-gapped or on-prem capacity so regulated data never crosses a border they do not control. Even surveillance vendors advertise edge processing as a selling point because sending every frame to the cloud is now both expensive and politically awkward.

The same window also produced enforcement reminders: a near-billion-euro GDPR fine against Uber in the Netherlands and a $400 million U.S. settlement with TikTok over children’s privacy. The message is consistent — jurisdiction and control over data are being enforced more visibly, not less.

The asymmetry at home

While governments race to keep AI on hardware they own, ambient commercial sensing is expanding in the other direction. A router that already sits in millions of living rooms can now report motion history to the provider’s cloud. Opt-in language does not change the architecture: the record leaves the house.

That asymmetry is the useful part of the story. Critical infrastructure and national systems are being designed so sensitive material stays local by default. Consumer systems still default to “phone home.” The technology to reverse that default for personal use already exists — local models, local graphs, apps that run as demos in the browser and as the real product on your machine.

The personal version of the same move

If a ministry will not put its workloads in someone else’s region, there is little reason an individual should put a lifetime of photos, notes, conversations, and agents in someone else’s chat log by default. Local-first apps are the individual answer to the same pressure: keep the data, run the model where you can, decide what leaves.

That is the design of the Sci-Fi Labs stack. The Knowledge Graph is a private spatial canvas for memory — photos, conversations, time as depth — built to run on hardware you control. Where is Paul? turns a personal travel record into a navigable globe and timeline without requiring the raw material to sit on a third-party server. The published sites are demos. The product is the copy that runs on your machine.

None of this requires waiting for a national AI fund. It requires treating personal data with the same seriousness the CNAS index is already applying to national data.
