Who has your data?

Your data is already tracked, sold, and resold by a dozen companies you cannot easily leave. OpenClaw is just the next door — every key you paste adds another one to the count.

The world you already live in

Before we talk about any agent, it is worth admitting the count it is competing with. You are already giving your data to all of these companies, every day, for free:

  • WhatsApp → Meta → Mark Zuckerberg. Every message, every contact, every group you are in, every photo you send, every location you share. WhatsApp’s metadata has fed Facebook’s ad systems for years; the same data now informs ads on Instagram and Facebook through Accounts Center. You are not Meta’s customer. You are the inventory.
  • Instagram, same. Who you look at, how long you look, what you save, what you almost buy. Meta holds nearly all of it and sells predictions about you to whoever bids.
  • Google is reading your email and listening to your phone. Gmail scans message content to shape ad and product profiles; Google Assistant and Android voice recognition ship your spoken queries to Google servers for transcription. Search, Maps, and Android hand them your location continuously.
  • YouTube knows what you almost believe. Every video you start, every one you finish, the two seconds you rewind, the one you skip at the 3-second mark — Google logs it all and uses it to shape the next row of recommendations. The watch graph is one of the most complete portraits of your curiosity, your boredom, your politics, and your 2 a.m. brain that any company has ever held.
  • Netflix knows what you like before you do. What you play, what you abandon after four minutes, which thumbnail made you click, which shows you rewatch. Netflix logs all of it, builds taste clusters from it, and uses those clusters to decide what to greenlight and which artwork to show you. You are not watching Netflix; Netflix is watching you watch Netflix.
  • Spotify has your mood on a timeline. The song you played on Monday morning, the one you put on loop after midnight, the playlist you built when you were sad — Spotify keeps all of it, and its algorithms use it to model your emotional state well enough to predict what you will want to hear next. That model is also a product, sold to advertisers who want to reach you in a specific mood.
  • Apple is listening too. Siri voice requests are sent to Apple for processing; the company’s own contractors have listened to recordings to grade the transcripts, and the legal fight over whether Siri accidentally recorded you is still live.
  • Amazon is listening. Every “Alexa” wake word — and a lot of near-misses — ships audio to AWS for processing. The recordings stay on Amazon’s servers unless you dig into settings to delete them.
  • The government is tracking where you go. In the U.S., cell-site location data and license-plate readers mean your movements are reconstructable without a warrant in most states. The FTC has formally found that major brokers sold precise location data that tracked people to sensitive places — clinics, places of worship, homes.

None of these companies asked you to build a profile. You built it for them, one tap and one play and one search at a time, and they kept it. The data does not sit in one place, either. It is sold, resold, blended, and resold again through a secondary market of data brokers — Acxiom, Experian, Oracle, LiveRamp, Epsilon — that most people have never heard of and none of us opted into. Your WhatsApp contact list, your Gmail contents, your Spotify mood graph, and your Netflix watch graph do not stay in the silo where you generated them. They get matched, joined into a single profile keyed to your name, device, and address, and sold to anyone who wants to bid on you: insurers, landlords, employers, political campaigns, and, above all, advertisers.

That last buyer — the ad market — is what makes the whole machine run, and the machine is not subtle. The algorithms do not just collect the data; they use it to try to sell you shit, continuously, and they have gotten very good at it. Every platform you use runs the same loop: watch what you do, model what you are likely to want next, and put that thing in front of you at the moment you are most likely to buy. YouTube finishes a video and queues up the one most likely to keep you watching — not the one most likely to be good for you. Netflix autoplays the next episode and rewrites the thumbnail to match the genres you click. Spotify ends a song and, if you are on the free tier, inserts an ad targeted at the mood it just measured. Instagram holds your thumb on a reel an extra half-second and learns exactly which body type, which product, which political framing keeps your eyes on the glass. Meta, Google, Amazon, and the brokers behind them feed all of that back into the ad auctions that follow you across the web — the shoes you looked at once show up in a banner on a news site three days later, not by accident, but because a model decided you were still warm. The system was not designed to inform you. It was designed to predict you, and the more of your data it holds, the better the prediction — and the harder it is to live outside of it.

So when the next product asks you to paste one more key, that is the world it is asking you to add to. You are not starting from zero. You are adding a line to a ledger that was already long.

Where OpenClaw fits in the ledger

OpenClaw is the easiest way to watch this happen, because OpenClaw is where it actually happened. The project started as Clawdbot — a personal weekend build that could read files, run shell commands, and called one model, Claude, to do anything more interesting than ls. One key, one company, one connection. The pitch was clean: the agent runs on your machine, it does things on your machine, and the only thing leaving the laptop is the prompt you typed and the reply that came back.

That is not what the setup screen looks like anymore. The wizard itself still only requires one key to start — the model key — but it then offers optional steps for web search and messaging channels, and the moment you say yes to any of them the prompts appear. Beyond the wizard, the fully-configured product can ask for a memory key to index your files, a web fetch key to read pages, a speech key for voice, a vision key for images. The messaging step alone offers ten channels: Slack, Discord, Telegram, WhatsApp, iMessage, Microsoft Teams, Feishu, QQ Bot, Signal, and Google Chat, each wanting its own bot token or login. The single door Clawdbot opened turned into a hallway of doors, and the onboarding flow treats every one of them as a reasonable install step.

Each key is a contract, and the contract is not just “let the agent call the API.” It is “let the provider see the traffic that flows through the API.” Your prompts, your transcripts, your images, your schedule, your friends’ phone numbers — whatever the key unlocks — now crosses a wire you do not control and lands on infrastructure you do not own. This is the part nobody puts on the landing page, so it is worth walking it key by key.

Count the keys

Privacy is not a switch; it is a ledger. The honest way to read a product’s privacy posture is to count the keys it can ask for and ask, for each one, what flows across it and who is on the other end. Here is that ledger for a present-day OpenClaw install — the wizard only forces the first row, but every row below it is one settings screen away:

Seven capabilities, each with its own key — and depending on which provider you pick for each, that is potentially a dozen different companies holding your data. The wizard only forces the first one; the rest are optional, but they are all one settings screen away, and the product does not work the same without them. None of them individually betrayed you. But the union of those datasets, held across a dozen houses you do not live in, is a more complete portrait of your life than any one of them could build alone — and you handed every piece over yourself, one paste at a time. Say goodbye to your privacy. It left through the doors you opened to make the agent convenient.

There is a second question hiding inside this ledger — which country owns the model you are talking to, and what that means for what you believe. It is big enough for its own piece: Who owns the answer?

The punchline is that it did not used to be this many doors. Clawdbot shipped with one key because it did one thing — talk to a model. Every key since then was added to give the agent a feature: memory that works, search that works, voice that works, channels that work. The features are real. So is the cost. Each key you add to unlock a capability also opens a direct line to a new company — and the connection stays open whether or not you are using the feature that day.

An agent that can ask for a key for every capability is not a local agent. It is a remote one with a local UI — and by OpenClaw’s present-day key count, it is a dozen remotes wearing one.

Why local-by-default inverts this

The Knowledge Graph stack is built on a different default: if a capability can run on your machine, it runs on your machine. Your voice is recognized on your own chip. Your images are understood on your own chip. Your notes and your memory are indexed on your own chip. Your files and your history live on your own disk. None of that leaves the laptop, because there is no company on the other end to send it to — the model is a file on your disk, the storage is a file you own.

The trade-off is that your laptop has limits, and the rest of the Knowledge Graph series is honest about them. Local costs memory, it costs battery, it costs the discipline of not reaching for a cloud service the moment a task gets hard. The payoff is that your information stays yours. Your prompts, your files, your voice, your photos do not cross a wire to a server you do not control. Privacy is not a promise a company made you — it is just what happens when nothing leaves the machine.

The honest position

This is not a religion. Your laptop cannot do everything a cloud server can, and when you hit something it genuinely cannot do, sending that one task out is a reasonable trade — eyes open, one thing at a time, never as the default path. The position worth defending is narrower and more useful:

  • Default to local. If the model fits on your machine, it runs on your machine. Your information stays on your disk until you have a real reason to send it somewhere. The count of companies holding your data stays at zero by default, not because they refused to take it, but because you never sent it.
  • One capability, one company — never bundled. The moment a product needs to hand a dozen different parts of your life to a dozen different companies to work properly, it is a cloud product with a local costume — and the costume is thin.
  • Count the connections, not the promises. Privacy is not what the privacy policy says. It is the set of wires your data actually flows through. Read the table, not the marketing.

The Knowledge Graph series is, among other things, an attempt to build a personal agent that does not require you to sign away the personal layer to make it work. The split between utility and personal AI matters here too: a utility agent reading your shell is one exposure profile, a personal agent reading your relationships is a much heavier one — and which one you are actually living in is decided by what leaves your machine.

Reclaiming your data is not a single act. It is the refusal, repeated every time a settings screen appears, to open a new door you do not need. The longer argument for that refusal is here.
