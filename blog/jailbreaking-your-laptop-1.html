Install Omarchy

This is part 1 of 5. You're building toward something bigger: you own the entire stack. The first step is the biggest — owning the OS.

You're building toward owning the whole stack — the computer, the AI on it, the site, the apps. The first step is the biggest: owning the operating system. You can run your own AI, publish your own site, and build your own apps without swapping the OS. People do. Changing the OS is what gives you full control — what apps run, what connects to the internet, how it looks. That's why you start here.

Take over the OS and you decide what runs. Way less stuff snooping on you. Only the processes you need to get things done. All those extra processes eat memory — memory you could use to run AI on the laptop itself.

So you swap it. The new one is called Omarchy. Copy your files off. Write the installer to a USB stick. Install. Restart.

If you're on a desktop — or only have a Bluetooth keyboard — plug in one with a cable. Bluetooth won't work when the computer first turns on. It can't type your password yet. People skip this, then they get stuck.

What you need

Backup your existing files first. Then run the install off the USB stick.

  • A laptop. 16 GB of memory is enough. Mac, Windows, or Linux — whatever you have.
  • A USB stick. 4 GB or bigger. The install file is under 6 GB.
  • A keyboard that works at boot. Laptop keys are fine. Desktop — or Bluetooth-only — needs a cable or a 2.4 GHz dongle. Bluetooth can't type the disk password yet.
  • The Omarchy install file. Download it from iso.omarchy.org. You'll put it on the USB stick in step 1.
  • An external hard drive. Copy your personal files here first so the install doesn't eat them — Desktop, Downloads, Documents, plus photos, videos, music, wherever you actually keep them.

Backup before you install

The install wipes the laptop. How long the copy takes depends on how much you have — photos, videos, music. Most people have a lot.

My machine had about 100 GB of that sitting on the disk. That's what I copied off.

For this step: copy the folders where your stuff actually lives. Desktop, Downloads, Documents — and Pictures, Movies, Music, or any other folder you dumped photos and videos into. Drag them yourself, or ask an AI on your machine to move them. Example below used OpenCode.

Plug the external drive in. Prompt:

Install Omarchy

Steps below are from the official Getting Started page. Do this part by hand. Backup is already done.

Before you start: if your photos, videos, music, and documents are still on the machine, stop. Copy them off (that's above). Desktop users: plug in a keyboard with a cable.
I'm on

  1. Download the install file and put it on a USB stick

    Get it from iso.omarchy.org. Under 6 GB. Write it to the stick with balenaEtcherwith balenaEtcherwith caligula. That stick is only for installing. When you're done, take it out.

  2. Turn off Secure Boot

    On a Mac you can skip this. Hold Option when you power on to get the boot menu, then pick the USB stick.Restart and tap F2, F12, or Del to get into the computer's settings. Turn off Secure Boot. If you see TPM, turn that off too. Omarchy won't install while Secure Boot is on.Restart and tap F2 or Del. If you see Secure Boot, turn it off. If the machine never had Windows, it may already be off.

  3. Boot off the USB stick

    Plug the stick in and turn the computer on. Hold Option until you see the boot menu, then pick the stick. Open the boot menu (often F12) and pick the stick. Pick the stick from the boot menu. You should land in the installer. If you don't, the stick didn't write right, or Secure Boot is still on.

  4. Choose your install type

    Two paths. Full-disk wipes the whole drive and Omarchy is the only system. Free-space puts it next to what you already have (dual boot). Keeping Windows? Turn off BitLocker first.Keeping macOS? Make empty space in Disk Utility first.Keeping your current Linux? Leave empty space on the drive. It encrypts the disk either way. Full-disk means the whole drive. That's why you copied files off first.

  5. Answer the configuration questions

    It asks for a computer name, your username, a password, and timezone. Pick a drive. On a fast machine it can finish in about a minute. Older hardware, still under five. You don't pick a bunch of extra software here. That's later, from the menu.

  6. Reboot into your new desktop

    Take the stick out when it says to. Type the disk password (laptop keys, or the cable keyboard if you're on a desktop). You should see a dark desktop, no dock, no icons. First time in, a small message pops up — click it for the list of keyboard shortcuts. Super is the Windows key, or Command on a Mac. Super + K is search for those shortcuts.

  7. Verify it worked

    Three checks. One: dark desktop, no dock, no icons. Two: Super + Space opens the menu. Three: Super + Return opens a terminal. If those work, you're in. If not, the note below.

If it did not boot: the three most common failures are simple to fix. Black screen or "Secure Boot violation": you did not turn off Secure Boot in step 2 — go back and disable it. Keyboard does not type at the password prompt: you're on a desktop with Bluetooth. Plug in a cable keyboard. Installed to the wrong drive or lost data: you picked the wrong drive in step 5 — this is why the steps say back up first. Reinstall to the correct drive.

That's the install. Seven steps. Here's what you do once you're in.

The keys that matter

Super + K is the one shortcut to remember. It shows the rest, searchable. You pick up the others by using it. Starter set below. Full list: hotkeys page.

KeysWhat it does
Super + KShow every desktop keybinding
Super + SpaceOmarchy menu. Apps, setup, install, update.
Super + Alt + SpaceApps only
Super + ReturnTerminal
Super + Shift + ReturnBrowser
Super + C / Super + VCopy and paste, including inside the terminal
Super + W or Super + QClose a window. The app actually quits. There's no dock to hide it in.

That last row is the one that catches former Mac users. On macOS, the red dot closes the window and keeps the app running in the dock. Omarchy has no dock. Close a window and the app is gone. If you wanted it minimized, you wanted it open. This is how tiling window managers work — there is no separate dock to minimize into. It takes about a day to stop reaching for a dock that is not there.

How tiling works

Omarchy uses a tiling layout called dwindle. Open one window, it takes the whole screen. Open a second, they split. Open a third, the active half splits again. You do not drag windows around. You do not fish one out from under another. The layout handles where things go. The navigation page covers the full set of movement and swap keys.

Workspaces are how you keep things separate. Super + 1 through Super + 4 (and beyond) jump to a workspace. The switch happens with a keybinding. No animation, no carousel, noExpose-style preview. You hit the number, you are there. Super + Shift + 1 sends the current window to workspace 1. That is the whole model. Windows tile within a workspace, workspaces switch with a number.

This is the part that feels strange for the first day and obvious by the third. You stop managing windows. You start naming what you want on screen and where.

What it looks like

It boots to a simple desktop. No dock of icons. You open windows from the keyboard. That's the default, and it's fine.

Some people go way further. They take over the entire look — colors, the bar, how windows move, the whole screen. Above and beyond. Same install you just did. These two are examples, to get you excited about what's possible. You don't have to. When you want to play with it, you can.

Install and update software

Hold Super (the Windows key, or Command on a Mac keyboard) and tap Space. That's the menu. Install apps from there. Update from there. What you can install.

To update: menu → Update → Omarchy. It saves a restore point first. If an update acts up.

If an update breaks it

Restart. At the boot menu, pick the restore point from before the update. You're back. More on that.

Those restore points bring back the system, not your photos and documents. That's why the files went to the external drive first.

You did it

You're on the new desktop. You're done with the hardest part. You own the OS. The first step was the biggest. The rest of the stack — AI on this laptop, your site, your apps — builds on this.
