Knowledge Graph, Part 2: Voice, mobile, and the queue

Why voice needed a GPU watchdog, why chat had to live on the canvas, and the optimizations that made multi-client local AI survivable.

Part 1 left a canvas and a pipeline. This part is about contact with reality: hands, microphones, concurrent clients, and the optimizations that turned a spatial prototype into something you could use every day.

Voice is infrastructure, not a button

Whisper on Metal was necessary and still not enough. After a few minutes idle, macOS evicts GPU residency sets. The next transcription hung. Health checks that only hit a CPU endpoint never warmed the GPU. The fix was a watchdog that forced real inference on a minimal WAV every ninety seconds, and restarted the server after consecutive failures.

On the client, recording became a lab notebook of failed theories. Press-and-hold, tap-to-record, double-tap for options, synthetic pointerup double-fires, iOS gesture chains broken when AudioContext.resume() ran before getUserMedia, HTTPS required for mic permission, crypto.randomUUID missing off localhost. Each commit was a response to a phone on the local network saying no.

HTTPS on the dev server was not polish. Without a secure context, the mic never initialized on a real device. The direction — voice-first memory — depended on that single constraint.

Chat leaves the sidebar

As long as chat lived in a separate panel, the canvas was a gallery and the AI was a different app. The direction changed when conversation became a spatial citizen: overlay on the graph, then a glowing mic orb, then conversation nodes — portrait cards with titles, active glow, camera fly-to when selected.

Architecture had to follow. Browser calls straight to llama-server do not survive refresh or a second device. Job tables, claim/cancel, an event bus with SSE and Postgres NOTIFY, a worker with multi-turn MCP tool loops, a client that reconnects with Last-Event-ID — the queue made the canvas multi-client without abandoning local models.

Streaming indicators moved onto conversation cards. You could record several voice notes without waiting for the model to finish. Queued responses kept the orb free. The optimization was not “faster tokens.” It was “voice stays ambient while the graph keeps working.”

Mobile as a first-class surface

Bottom sheets, safe-area insets, 44px targets, timeline pills that did not wrap. Pinch and pan were rewritten to match a reference infinite-canvas feel; drift was disabled on touch so the camera stopped sliding after a gesture. The timeline picker evolved from a dropdown into an iOS-style wheel with continuous offset and snap-on-settle. Touch lag, boundary resets, accidental opens — sanded down until the spatial metaphor held in a pocket.

Libraries that made contact survivable

  • svelte-gesturesPinch, pan, swipe — touch that matches the infinite-canvas reference
  • MCP TypeScript SDKTool calls so the local model can read and write the graph
  • whisper.cppHost Metal transcription with a real-inference keepalive
  • FastAPILLM job queue, SSE streams, multi-client broadcast
  • three.jsConversation nodes and fly-to camera on the same canvas as photos
  • Vite · basic-sslHTTPS in dev so mobile mic permission works

By early August the system could ingest photos, answer from the graph, take voice, and stay coherent across clients — still fully local. The remaining work was less make it work and more make it feel inevitable.
