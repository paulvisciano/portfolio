South Beach throughout the years

A SoBe circle. Three miles. You can argue where South Beach ends; Lincoln Road is a clean cutoff. The phone logged the loop: 444 Ocean Drive, Puerto Sagua, Sunburn on Alton, then the mall and back down the sand.

Miami Beach was a mangrove sandbar until Carl Fisher and a handful of other boosters dredged, planted, and sold it as a winter city. A century later the southern tip still refuses to pick a decade. We walked it as a circle: Ocean Drive at the south end, Collins for coffee, Alton for the shop with the wall, Lincoln Road as the northern lid, then back down the beach. Three miles. Two hours. Three pins.

The clock on the phone is the document. Left 444 Ocean Drive at 10:20. Puerto Sagua four minutes later. Sunburn on Alton at 11:01. The rest is sidewalk.

1962 · 700 CollinsPuerto Sagua

The placemat on the granite still prints the address, the fish, and the year: 700 Collins Avenue, established 1962. The name is not a mood. Puerto Sagua is a port — Sagua la Grande, Villa Clara, the river town Cubans call La Villa del Undoso. The Rivero family left an original restaurant of the same name in Havana when the Revolution nationalized it. Grandfather and two sons rebuilt the counter here. David Rivero still runs it. His son Alex works the floor. In April 2026 the city read a proclamation for more than sixty years of the same job on the same corner.

A kitchen fire on July 31, 2015, closed the room for six months. About $150,000 in damage. Regulars came back the night it reopened. The Havana street-scene paintings — the ones customers still point at and name the man in black — came down so they would survive the rebuild. That is the building’s whole plot: it has been allowed to stay itself. Breakfast specials until 11. Phone number still (305) 673-1115. You sit at the counter if you can.

If you only have one hour in South Beach, spend twenty minutes of it at a ventanita or a counter that has been pouring coffee longer than you have been visiting.

1909–1930s · Ocean DriveLummus Park and the hotels that face it

The turquoise Buick is parked on Ocean Drive with Lummus Park on the east side — palms, the low restroom pavilion, the Atlantic behind the hedge. The park is named for the Lummus brothers, J.N. and J.E., early Beach boosters. It runs from 5th Street to 14th Place, a grass strip that became the front lawn of the Deco hotels. When the district was rebuilt in the mid-1980s the wavy promenade was laid in as a cousin of Roberto Burle Marx’s Copacabana walk. Film crews have used this exact sidewalk since Miami Vice. The cars are not a set. Someone parks them because the street still lets them.

The low hotels across the asphalt are the ones that almost vanished. Between about 1925 and 1942, Henry Hohauser, L. Murray Dixon, and Albert Anis filled a square mile with three- and four-story winter hotels: eyebrows over the windows, ships and sunbursts in the plaster, neon later. After the war the Beach went taller — Lapidus, Fontainebleau, cheap flights. By the late 1970s these little buildings were SROs. Barbara Baer Capitman and the Miami Design Preservation League stopped the wrecking ball. May 14, 1979: the Miami Beach Architectural District on the National Register, first twentieth-century historic district in the country. That is why a 1950 Buick can still line up with a 1930s porch.

The blue Volkswagen sits on a side street facing a white Streamline block with a residential-parking sign. Same hardware, quieter block. The peach tower we photographed is the other scale: twelve-plus stories, two tile-roof corner towers, a two-story arched base, window units in half the openings, an ArtDecoHotels.com plate on the wall. We did not catch a name on the lintel. The silhouette is the story anyway — a late-1920s / 1930s apartment hotel that grew taller than Ocean Drive, kept the Mediterranean roof, and never got the renovation that turns a building beige.

2014–2026 · Every poleThe park is also a sensor

Walk the same grass as the Buick and you notice the hardware that did not exist on the 1940s postcards. The saucer lights on Lummus Park are not just lights. Mid-pole: a white dome. Sometimes a second box. Sometimes a laminated sign that says the city values its outdoor spaces and you should not trespass. The camera does not need the sign. It is already working.

Miami Beach Police started a camera program in 2014 with twenty-five fixed cameras, four license-plate readers, and a cubicle. They now run a Real-Time Intelligence Center. The department’s own numbers, as of 2025–26: more than 850 cameras, then more than 1,100 devices and about 2,000 views; roughly thirty to forty license-plate readers from Motorola and Axon, not Flock. The network began on Ocean Drive and Lummus — the exact strip of this walk — then Collins, then Washington. After a chaotic spring break they doubled the cameras on Ocean Drive and added more on Beachwalk. Drones as first responder launched in 2025. The same dunes that used to hide only sea grapes now get aerial passes.

This week the argument is not whether the poles exist. It is who gets to keep the plate readers. Tallahassee ordered ALPRs off state roads. The mayor says he will fight to keep them, or move them onto hotel and condo property. Venetian Islands HOAs already run their own Flock units at the inbound gates. The walk does not resolve the policy. It only records what you can see without a badge: every few palms, a dome. The 1930s hotels face the park. The park faces a room downtown that can pull the feed before a cruiser arrives.

If you have lived here a long time you remember when the only camera on this grass was a tourist’s. That is the current layer. Same sidewalk. New job.

1936 · 444 Ocean DriveThe south pin

The Timeline starts at 444 Ocean Drive. A four-story hotel, built 1936, twenty-seven rooms, same owner as 436 next door. Ocean Beach Florida Subdivision — the Lummus brothers’ 1912 plat, the first street grid on this sandbar. This is the south end of the Deco strip, before the numbers get famous. Left at 10:20. Five minutes west to the counter.

1940 / 2024 · 1428 AltonSunburn

After the coffee the circle turns inland. Thirty-one minutes, 1.1 miles, to 1428 Alton Road — west side, between 14th and 15th, two blocks south of Lincoln. The shell is a 1940 one-story commercial building. The tenant is newer. Sunburn opened here in April 2024 as its first Miami-Dade store, a Florida-only operator that bought MedMen’s state assets and put a beach-house interior on Alton instead of another Collins MSO box.

The name is the family story. Founder Brady Cobb spent a decade in Washington on cannabis law, then built a Florida-only operator. His father, Clyde Walton “Bill” Cobb, was a Key West saltwater cowboy in the 1970s — the kind of Florida history this city usually leaves off the walking tour. The son’s version is licensed and sitting on the west edge of the circle. Florida is still a medical state. The tourists can see the sign. They cannot use it.

1979–1991 · Inside the shopThe 80s are on a wall now

The glory-days wall is in that room. Waterskiers. Ocean Drive when the cars were the traffic, not the prop. A peloton rolling past a woman in a dotted bikini. Dolphins jerseys. Cheap acid jokes. Flags.

The wall is doing what the district almost failed to do: keep the operating system. South Beach in the late 70s was written off. Crime was up. Lincoln Road emptied. Then two rescues arrived at once — the historic designation of 1979, and a television show that turned the pastel hotels into a national brand. Miami Vice started in 1984. Artists had already moved onto Lincoln because the rent had given up. Models walked dogs after jobs. The neon arrived as a rescue, not a brand guide. Then the brand guide won.

We have lived enough of those years to recognize the difference between nostalgia and archive. A lot of this wall is both. Look long enough and you stop treating the 80s as a filter. Same hardware. Different software. The beach is still the beach. The question is who gets to stand on it without performing.

1912–1960 · Lincoln RoadThe Fifth Avenue of the South

Carl Fisher cut Lincoln Road out of mangrove in 1912 and named it for Abraham Lincoln — a northern name on a southern sandbar, one year before Miami put up its Confederate memorial. He wanted a Fifth Avenue for the winter city. His own house sat at the ocean end. His real-estate office sat on the road. By the late 1920s the claim was not a slogan. Saks Fifth Avenue opened a three-story shop in 1928 and staged fashion shows. Bonwit Teller, Burdines, Elizabeth Arden, Cadillac and Packard showrooms, jewelers, white gloves. Ann Armbruster later called it “the public center of swank.” Making a purchase was for the wealthy. For everyone else it was an occasion.

The postcards from the 1930s and 40s still show the hardware: two-way traffic, parked cars on both curbs, Mediterranean and Streamline blocks, palms in a center strip. Woolworth and Liggett’s on the Washington Avenue corner. Colony Theatre’s red marquee already up at Lenox. Saks under a white canopy. In the war years the Army Air Forces Technical Training Command marched the same asphalt — hotels had become barracks, Lincoln Road a parade ground.

By the mid-1950s the heyday had leaked north. Fontainebleau and Eden Roc put the shops inside the hotels. Suburban malls did the rest. Merchants and the city asked Morris Lapidus — already the architect of too much — to save the street. He said a car never bought anything. May 1959: he unveiled the plan. November 1959: voters passed a $600,000 bond. November 28, 1960: grand opening of Lincoln Road Mall, one of the first open-air pedestrian malls in the country. Fountains, shade follies, a tram from Washington to Alton. “Too much is never enough.”

The mall did not hold. Late 70s: vacancies, a ghost town letter to the Miami News, more cattle egrets than pigeons. What brought it back was not another architect. Ellie Schneiderman’s South Florida Art Center (1984) took twenty-two empty stores and put seventy artists in them. Miami City Ballet opened a storefront studio in 1986 so you could watch class through the glass. Colony reopened. New World Symphony took the Lincoln Theatre in 1989. By the early 90s it was the living room — models walking dogs, skaters at cafes, Books & Books. Then the chains arrived, the rents went up, and the street became what Fisher had asked for in the first place: a luxury mall that still refuses cars. National Register, May 6, 2011.

2010 · Lincoln & Alton1111 Lincoln Road

At the west end the current landmark is a parking garage.

Developer Robert Wennett bought the 1968 SunTrust Brutalist tower and the surface lot next to it in 2005. Most people told him to tear the bank down. He kept it and hired Herzog & de Meuron — the Bird’s Nest office — to build a garage that would not look like a garage. Christine Binswanger ran the design. Construction 2008–2010. About $65 million. Seven stacked slabs on tilted columns, 300 cars, shops on the ground, a penthouse on top. The city had to grant extra height. The first week, Wennett called the architects: people could not tell what the building was, and the ones who could thought it was unfinished. That is still the compliment.

This summer Padel X Summer Club painted LINCOLN RD on outdoor courts in the garage’s shadow — June 20 through mid-September 2026. Sport as furniture. You can watch a point without a reservation.

1955 · 400 block407 Lincoln, the Clock Tower

The glass slab behind the bronze animals is 407 Lincoln Road. Architect Edwin T. Reeder, 1955, Postwar Modern / MiMo. Built as the tower for Miami Beach Federal Savings and Loan — later Financial Federal. Thirteen stories, still the tallest office building on the mall. Original skin: staggered turquoise glass and stamped aluminum. In 1987 the city let them swap the turquoise for the dark reflective blue still there. The four-sided rooftop clock went up in 1956 and flipped TIME / TEMPERATURE. Parents told kids to be home before it read 10. Locals just called it the Clock Building.

In front of it this year: Gillie and Marc’s Wild Couch Party, installed July 2025 on the 400 block through July 2026. Forty feet of bronze, more than 13,000 pounds. Dogman, Rabbitwoman, a giraffe, a rhino, a gorilla, a koala. Sit on it. That is the commission.

1935 · 1040 LincolnColony Theatre

The striped house behind the fountain opened January 25, 1935, as a Paramount movie palace — 1,200 seats, billed as Miami Beach’s “Beauty Queen.” Opening night ran Clive of India. Carole Lombard was on the guest list. During World War II, when the Beach hotels were barracks, the Colony screened training, health, and newsreels for GIs. Morris Lapidus’s 1960 mall remodel moved the entrance toward Lenox. The city took it over; a 1980s conversion made it a cultural room; a $6.5 million Save America’s Treasures restoration by R.J. Heisenbottle, finished in 2006, put the Deco face and marquee back. Since 2016 Miami New Drama has been in residence. Four hundred and seventeen seats now. The mosaic fountain in the plaza is still the best place to stand still.

Over the fountain’s right shoulder is Frank Gehry’s New World Center, opened January 2011 for Michael Tilson Thomas’s New World Symphony — the orchestra he co-founded in 1987. Gehry used to babysit Tilson Thomas in Los Angeles. The hall cost about $160 million. The colored glass box is the 21st-century civic building on the same block as the 1935 movie house.

1921 · 1620 DrexelMiami Beach Community Church

Two minutes off the mall, the lintel still says the name. Founded 1921 on land Carl Fisher donated. First church on Miami Beach. Architect Walter De Garmo — first registered architect in Miami — designed the Mediterranean Revival facade: scrolled pediment, rose window, twisted columns. For years the growing city used it as social hall as much as sanctuary. In 1957 the congregation joined the United Church of Christ. Start the history here if you want the island before the postcard. Finish the coffee first if you want to understand why people stayed.

2006 / 2015 · 1021 LincolnThe Apple Store

Apple’s first Lincoln Road shop opened July 21, 2006, at 738 Lincoln, inside a tired former Chrysler showroom. In April 2015 they moved to 1021 Lincoln, a new building by Miami’s Touzet Studio that was required to remember the 1920s–30s auto showrooms on this street: keystone panels, a glass front, the scale of the neighbors. The old facade fight with preservationists is why the new store looks like a showroom instead of a mall box. The watch table in the photograph is the newest counter on a street that has sold cars, film tickets, savings accounts, and café con leche.

If you visit, walk it as a timeline

Not a complete list. A local list. The kind you follow if you already know Ocean Drive exists and you would like the day to feel like history you can stand in, not a circuit.

  • 1962 · Coffee · Collins & 7th Puerto Sagua Counter if you can. Café con leche, maybe toast. Breakfast specials until 11. 700 Collins Avenue. Open since 1962, which on this island is a civic achievement.
  • 1930s · Walk · morning or golden hour Collins into Ocean Drive Look at the hotels from the sidewalk, not from a party-bus window. This is the district Capitman saved in 1979. The parked classics are part of the architecture now. Photograph them. Then put the phone away for a block.
  • 1921–2026 · Living room · daylight Lincoln Road Mall Colony Theatre (1935) and the fountain. Clock Tower at 407 Lincoln — sit on the Wild Couch Party if it is still out. 1111 Lincoln for the 2010 garage and a padel point. Step off to the Community Church on Drexel for 1921.
  • Now · Alton · 14th–15th Sunburn, 1428 Alton Road The west pin of the circle. 1940 storefront, Florida-only shop since April 2024. The 80s wall is inside. Medical card required. Or skip it and keep walking the loop.
  • 2026 · Look up The poles on Lummus The saucer lights are also cameras. Ocean Drive and the park were the first targets of a network that now runs more than a thousand devices into a Real-Time Intelligence Center. Notice them. Then decide how you feel about the feed.
  • Do not Only do the night strip Ocean Drive after 11 is a product. Lincoln Road at 10 in the morning is a neighborhood that still remembers itself. Sunday farmers market if you catch it.
The city will keep remixing the same ten blocks. The job, if you live here, is to notice which layer is still load-bearing.

We have both lived here throughout the years. That is the whole credential. Not a guidebook. A pair of people who know what the coffee is supposed to taste like, and who still get quiet when a turquoise Buick lines up with a row of palms and the light does the thing it has been doing since before either of us arrived.

This walk is also a pin. Where is Paul? exists so a day like this does not vanish into the camera roll. The globe is the long version. These photographs are the short one.
