Thoughts on Skill Erosion

Autonomy is a balance. Give the machine more of it and you keep less. Driving took decades to hand over. AI does not wait that long.

No steering wheel. No pedals. Tesla’s Cybercab started paid rides in Austin on September 3 with nothing for a human to take over.1 That is not a styling choice. That is letting go of control, built into the seat.

I keep turning that over. Autonomy is not a pile that grows on both sides. It is a balance. The more the machine has, the less you have. We call it convenience. It is also a transfer of freedom.

It took decades to drop the wheel

The tech was on public roads a long time before anyone would sit in a cabin with nothing to grab. Google started the self-driving car project in 2009. In October 2010 Sebastian Thrun posted that the cars had already driven from Mountain View to Santa Monica, down Lombard Street, across the Golden Gate, around Tahoe — more than 140,000 miles, a person still in the seat in case the software needed one.2 Nevada issued the first U.S. license for a self-driven car in May 2012.3

That was about fifteen years ago. The cars could already drive. What was missing was trust, law, and a public that would accept a ride with no way to take over.

Google designed that ride anyway. In May 2014 Chris Urmson wrote that the prototype would have no steering wheel, no accelerator, no brake pedal, because the software and sensors would do all the work.4 They nicknamed it Firefly. In October 2015 a legally blind passenger, Steve Mahan, took a public-road trip in one with no controls and no human backup.5

Waymo still runs most of its paid fleet with a wheel in the car. Amazon’s Zoox does not. NHTSA let Zoox charge for rides in a cabin with no steering wheel and no pedals this summer.6 Tesla wants the no-wheel cab to be a large one. Right now it is a small Austin geofence and a federal audit of how they certified a car with nothing to grab.7

You do not let go without trust

Psychologists and human-factors people have been writing this down for forty years. Lisanne Bainbridge called it the irony of automation in 1983: the more the system does, the worse you get at the leftover job of watching it, and the worse you are when you have to take over.8 Physical skill decays when you stop using it. So does the feel for what the machine is doing.

John Lee and Katrina See defined trust in automation as the attitude that an agent will help you in a situation where you are uncertain and vulnerable.9 That is the Cybercab. You are in a two-seat gold coupe with no controls. Trust is the only reason you close the door.

People feel that. A 2022 study found they rated a fully automated car as less safe than a highly automated one even after they were shown the safety numbers. The path was not missing information. It was perceived lack of control, then anxiety.10 No wheel reads as no way out. That is why it took so long. Not because nobody had built a car that could drive.

The skill is already leaving

I said the next generations could be the first that do not know how to drive. That is the direction. It already started without robotaxis.

In 1983 about half of U.S. 16-year-olds had a license. By 2022 it was about a quarter. Eighteen-year-olds went from about 80% licensed to about 60%.11 Uber, cost, cities, phones. The license stopped being the default door to freedom. A cab with no wheel makes not-driving the product, not a delay.

All cars will not be self-driving next year. Mixed fleets last a long time. If the cabs work, driving becomes optional, then rare, then a hobby. A basic human skill does not vanish in a press release. It vanishes when the next kid never needs it.

We already handed off the map

The historical parallel is not typewriters. It is GPS.

Dahmani and Bohbot tracked regular drivers and then retested a smaller group three years later. More lifetime GPS use went with worse spatial memory when people had to navigate without it. The people who used GPS more between tests declined more. They were not using it because they already felt lost. The offload came first.12 Follow the arrow and the hippocampus does less work. That is skill erosion you can measure.

Calculators did a milder version of the same thing to number sense. Phones did it to remembering a number. None of that arrived as a crisis. It arrived as a default. You notice when the default is gone and you cannot do the old job.

Over-reliance is the habit

This is already how a lot of a day works. Turn-by-turn to the grocery store you have visited a hundred times. A rating instead of a judgment. A chatbot instead of a first draft. A feed instead of a walk. Each one is small. Together they are a habit of not knowing how.

Reliance is not the same as use. Use is a tool in the hand. Reliance is when the tool is the only way the task exists for you. Bainbridge’s operator watching a plant he can no longer stabilize. A driver who cannot find home without the blue line. A writer who cannot start a paragraph without a prompt. Same shape.

Self-driving made the trade visible because the wheel is an object. You can photograph its absence. AI is quieter. The skill that leaves is judgment, recall, the ability to sit with a hard sentence. Nobody sends a press release when that atrophies.

AI is the fast version

We spent decades getting comfortable with a car that drives. We are spending months getting comfortable with a model that writes, codes, remembers, and answers. The psychology is the same. Trust, then let go, then forget you used to do it.

The difference is speed, and who holds the other side of the balance. A cab still dumps you at a curb. A model that owns the answer can shape what you believe on the way there. I wrote about that in Who owns the answer?

I am not arguing you should refuse the ride. I take tools. I write with them. The point is to see the trade while you still have a choice. Keep a skill because you might need it, and because a life where the machine has all the autonomy is a life where you have less.

The wheel was the obvious one. The chat box is the one on this desk.
