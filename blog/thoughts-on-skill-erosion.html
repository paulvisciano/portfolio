Thoughts on Skill Erosion

The more that the machines have autonomy, the less that we have. In a way we're giving away our freedom.

No pedals or wheels

That checks. Google started in 2009. October 2010, Sebastian Thrun: Mountain View to Santa Monica, Lombard, the Golden Gate, Tahoe. 140,000 miles. A person still in the seat.1 Nevada licensed it in 2012.2 Fifteen years ago from this week is about right.

They even built the no-wheel car. Firefly, 2014. No steering wheel, no pedals.3 A blind passenger rode one on public roads in 2015 with nothing to grab.4

Waymo's paid Jaguars still have a wheel. Zoox does not.5 Tesla Cybercab, Austin, September 3. Small geofence. They want it to be one of the large ones. It isn't yet.6

You need to trust it in order to let go

Lisanne Bainbridge was a cognitive psychologist at University College London. Factories, process plants, cockpits. In 1983 she published a short paper, Ironies of Automation. Operators who only watch. Same shape as a cab with nothing to grab.

Lisanne Bainbridge · Ironies of Automation · 1983

This paper suggests that the increased interest in human factors among engineers reflects the irony that the more advanced a control system is, so the more crucial may be the contribution of the human operator.

Unfortunately, physical skills deteriorate when they are not used, particularly the refinements of gain and timing. This means that a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be an inexperienced one.

There is some concern that the present generation of automated systems, which are monitored by former manual operators, are riding on their skills, which later generations of operators cannot be expected to have.

Automatica 19(6), 775–779

Skill dies when you stop using it. The next kid never had it to lose.

A 2022 study showed this. Give people a car they can't take over, and they call it less safe than one that still has a wheel — even after you show them the numbers. They weren't missing data. They were missing control, and that made them anxious.7 No wheel reads as no way out. That's why it took decades. Not because nobody had built a car that could drive.

A basic human skill

That's the direction. It already started. In 1983 about half of U.S. 16-year-olds had a license. By 2022 it was about a quarter.8 Uber, cost, cities, phones. A cab with no wheel makes not-driving the product, not a delay.

Not every car next year. If the cabs work, driving becomes optional, then rare, then a hobby. A basic human skill does not vanish in a press release. It vanishes when the next kid never needs it.

There will always be rebels. People who refuse the cab and keep steering muscle cars.

Now with AI

We already let go of a lot of things. The map. The phone number. Now the sentence.

The wheel was the visible one. You can photograph its absence. AI is quieter. Trust, then let go, then forget you used to do it. Months, not decades.

I take tools. I write with them. The point is to see the trade while you still have a choice. Keep a skill because you might need it, and because a life where the machine has all the autonomy is a life where you have less.
