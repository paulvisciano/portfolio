Thoughts on Skill Erosion

The more that the machines have autonomy, the less that we have. In a way we're giving away our freedom.

There will always be rebels. Mustang drivers. People in old-school cars who refuse the Cybercab and keep both hands on a wheel.

No pedals or a steering wheel

Google started in 2009 under Sebastian Thrun, a Stanford professor whose driverless car had already won DARPA's desert race. By October 2010 he had 140,000 miles on public roads — Mountain View to Santa Monica, Lombard, the Golden Gate, Tahoe — with a person still in the seat.1 Nevada licensed it in 2012.2 That was about 15 years ago to this day.

They even built the no-wheel car. Firefly, 2014. Google designed it from scratch as a two-seater with no steering wheel, no pedals, and a button to start. You sat. About a hundred of them, capped at 25 miles an hour.3 On October 20, 2015, Steve Mahan — legally blind — rode one through an Austin neighborhood on public roads.4

Ten years later Waymo came back to Austin. On March 4, 2025, they opened to the public: Jaguar I-PACEs on Uber, 37 square miles, still with a steering wheel.5 People wanted in. You couldn't order a Waymo on purpose — only tick a box in Uber and hope. Watching one arrive is a kind of magic. No driver. It pulls up and you get in the back. Then it takes off and the wheel turns by itself — fear, excitement, the future is here.6 On September 3, 2026, Tesla Cybercabs were unleashed on Austin. Same streets. No driver, and no wheel to watch.7

You need to trust it in order to let go

This is a social experiment at city scale. Not a lab. Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Austin. Get a population comfortable enough to let go of the wheel, then take the wheel out. The technology was ready years ago. Trust was not.

The companies know. Waymo's pitch is trust — they call the software the world's most trusted driver. They hire human-factors engineers, UX researchers, ethnographers. The job is to make the empty seat feel normal. Tesla has a Human Factors team. The video that tells you not to touch the wheel, the Uber toggle, the early-rider waitlist — that's not the car. That's getting people to surrender control.10

Lisanne Bainbridge was a cognitive psychologist at University College London. She studied operators in factories, process plants, and cockpits. In 1983 she published a short paper, Ironies of Automation. Human factors is the engineering name for how people actually behave around machines. She was watching operators whose only job was to monitor a process they no longer ran, and to take over when the automation failed. What she found: the more you automate, the more that leftover person matters — and the worse they get at taking over.

Lisanne Bainbridge · Ironies of Automation · 1983

This paper suggests that the increased interest in human factors among engineers reflects the irony that the more advanced a control system is, so the more crucial may be the contribution of the human operator.

Unfortunately, physical skills deteriorate when they are not used, particularly the refinements of gain and timing. This means that a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be an inexperienced one.

There is some concern that the present generation of automated systems, which are monitored by former manual operators, are riding on their skills, which later generations of operators cannot be expected to have.

Automatica 19(6), 775–779

The irony: you build a system to get rid of the human, and the leftover human — now the only backup — can no longer do the job. Watching is not practice. Gain and timing — how hard to push, when — go first. The operator who used to be good is a beginner the day they have to take over. The plants in 1983 still had people who had done it by hand. The machines were borrowing those old skills. Later operators never had them. Skill dies when you stop using it.

In a car, taking the wheel out is that last step. There is no take-over. A 2022 study gave people that car. They called it less safe than one that still had a wheel — even after they saw the numbers. They weren't missing data. They were missing control, and that made them anxious.8

That's the price. You let go because the cabin feels safe. You stop practicing. The next kid never learns, and you can't buy the skill back. Then one day we wake up and people can't drive.

A basic human skill

In 1983 about half of U.S. 16-year-olds had a license. By 2022 it was about a quarter.9 Uber, cost, cities, phones. A Cybercab with no wheel makes not-driving the product, not a delay.

If the Cybercabs work, driving becomes optional, then rare, then a hobby. Except the rebels in the Mustangs.

What do you do if the car takes you to jail and there's no wheel to turn? What do you do if you're about to crash and you can't swerve? If your kids never learn to drive, can they ever leave without asking a machine? If a city forgets how to drive, who is still free to leave?
